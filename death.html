Death and Legacy

Kurt Cobain died by suicide on April 5, 1994, at his home in Seattle — a self-inflicted gunshot wound. He was 27. His body was found three days later, on April 8.

What led up to it

The last months were hard. On March 4, 1994, he was hospitalized in Rome after an overdose; friends and family confronted him about his drug use and urged him to get help. He agreed and entered a rehab program in Los Angeles, but left it on April 1 and returned to Seattle.

By then he had spent years living with depression, with stomach pain that doctors could not explain, and with a heroin addiction. He had been open about all of it. That does not make what happened easier to understand — it only means the warning signs were visible.

After

On April 10, 1994, thousands gathered for a public vigil at Seattle Center, and Courtney Love read passages from his letter to the crowd.

The note

The note he left was addressed to Boddah — the imaginary friend of his childhood. In it he wrote that he no longer felt the excitement of listening to music or creating it, that he loved his wife and daughter more than anything, and he closed with a line borrowed from Neil Young: "it's better to burn out than to fade away." Those are the facts of it. We do not reprint the whole letter here — some things belong to the people they were written for.

Cobain is often listed among the "27 Club," the musicians who died at that age — Jimi Hendrix, Janis Joplin, Jim Morrison. But reducing his life to that number is unfair. He was a songwriter, a musician, a father, and a person who did not make it.

Legacy

After his death Nirvana ended. Dave Grohl formed Foo Fighters; Krist Novoselic went on to his own projects. The songs stayed — and kept finding new listeners born long after 1994.

Cobain's death pushed people to talk more openly about depression and addiction. His story did not become a "lesson" — but it did bring forward subjects that had been kept quiet.

The romanticization

One more fact, stated plainly: after his death, the culture turned him into mythology. The "27 Club." The "burn out" line printed on t-shirts. His face on posters in dorm rooms of people who never heard the music. That mythology is not him, and it is not the songs. It is what happens when a person becomes a product. This site tries to keep the songs, and leave the product alone.

I never met him. I only know the songs — and the songs know me.

April 5th isn't a date I mark on a calendar. It's a weight that sits there quietly. Every year people still remember him: flowers at his old house in Seattle, candles in their own rooms. I leave mine here, in this little corner of the internet, because it is the only place I have.

in memory of Kurt Cobain · 1967–1994
