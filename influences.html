Kurt's Influences

the music he carried inside him, in his own words where possible

Kurt made lists. He kept notebooks full of his favorite albums, ranked them, re-ranked them, crossed things out and rewrote them. He gave interviewers long answers about bands most people in the room had never heard of. He believed in the music that influenced him more than he believed in his own.

This page is about where Nirvana came from.

The Pixies

Kurt said it plainly in 1994: "I was trying to write the ultimate pop song. I was basically trying to rip off the Pixies. I should credit that influence." He was talking about "Smells Like Teen Spirit."

The Pixies' formula — quiet verse, loud chorus, the dynamic shift treated as its own kind of violence — runs through most of Nirvana's catalog. Kurt heard Surfer Rosa (1988) and Doolittle (1989) at a time when his own songwriting was just starting to crystallize. The Pixies showed him that pop structure and noise were not opposites.

Black Francis (Frank Black) of the Pixies has said in interviews that he was not particularly aware of Kurt's feelings about the band until after Kurt died. He seemed moved by this.

Lead Belly

Huddie William Ledbetter — Lead Belly — was a blues and folk singer from Louisiana who recorded in the 1930s and 1940s and died in 1949. Kurt was obsessed with him.

The connection runs through Unplugged: "Where Did You Sleep Last Night" (also known as "In the Pines") was a Lead Belly song, and Kurt chose to close the entire performance with it. He stretched the final note until it cracked. It is one of the most famous moments in the history of MTV.

What Kurt heard in Lead Belly was rawness without pretension. Lead Belly did not care about being polished. He cared about the song. Kurt recognized this as something worth preserving.

"I love Lead Belly. His music is so raw and pure. I feel the same way about him as I do about Daniel Johnston — it's completely sincere, completely honest music."
— Kurt Cobain, 1993

The Melvins

The Melvins were from Montesano, Washington — twenty minutes from Aberdeen. They played slow, heavy, deliberately difficult music that combined Black Sabbath and hardcore punk in a way that nobody else was doing in 1983. Kurt was seventeen when he discovered them. He roadied for them. He auditioned for them. He never got the job.

What the Melvins gave him was the idea that heaviness was not about speed. Their songs crawled. They sat on a riff until it became something else. The willingness to be slow and uncomfortable shows up all over Bleach and in songs like "Floyd the Barber" and "Blew."

Buzz Osbourne (King Buzzo) of the Melvins remained one of Kurt's closest friends until the end. He was one of the people who introduced Kurt to Krist Novoselic.

Sonic Youth

Sonic Youth were the bridge between the New York art underground and the American alternative scene. They had been making records since 1981 — records that treated guitar tuning as a compositional choice, that used the studio as an instrument, that cared about noise and beauty at the same time.

Their 1988 album Daydream Nation was the album. Kurt said it in multiple interviews. It showed him that you could be independent, uncompromising, and also make something that felt necessary rather than merely confrontational.

Thurston Moore and Kim Gordon of Sonic Youth were personally supportive of Nirvana during the early days, before anyone else was paying attention. They recommended Nirvana to DGC Records. This is not a small thing.

What Sonic Youth showed Nirvana
that you could sign to a major and not sound major
that guitar noise was a melody, not just texture
that it was possible to be weird and also important
that independence was a sound, not just a business model

Neil Young

The press called Kurt "the godfather of grunge." Kurt called Neil Young "the godfather of grunge." He meant it. Young had been making raw, distorted, country-influenced guitar music since 1969 on Everybody Knows This Is Nowhere and through Crazy Horse. He was also still doing it in 1992. Harvest Moon and Ragged Glory both came out within a few years of Nevermind.

In his suicide note, Kurt quoted Young: "it's better to burn out than to fade away." The line is from "Hey Hey, My My (Into the Black)," 1979. Young has said he was disturbed by this — that the lyric was not meant to be a philosophy, that he regrets what it became.

"I love Neil Young so much. I love his guitar tones. I love his songs. I think he has one of the greatest voices I've ever heard."
— Kurt Cobain, Rolling Stone, 1994

Daniel Johnston

Daniel Johnston was a musician and artist from Austin, Texas who made home recordings on a boombox cassette player and distributed them himself. The recordings were lo-fi almost past the point of functionality. The songs were about love, demons, Casper the Friendly Ghost, and the terror of being alive. They were also, underneath everything, beautiful.

Kurt wore a Daniel Johnston t-shirt — a hand-drawn frog, from Johnston's self-made artwork — to the 1992 MTV Video Music Awards. The shirt was visible in multiple photographs. Johnston's distribution tripled overnight. Kurt later said this was one of the most deliberate things he ever did with his fame.

What Johnston represented for Kurt was proof that sincerity was enough. That you didn't need production, distribution, marketing, or permission. That a song made in your bedroom on a forty-dollar cassette recorder could reach someone who needed it.

The Vaselines

The Vaselines were a Scottish band who existed for about two years (1986–1989) and recorded one album. Kurt covered two of their songs: "Molly's Lips" and "Jesus Doesn't Want Me for a Sunbeam," which appears on MTV Unplugged. Frances Bean Cobain is named after Frances McKee, the singer of The Vaselines.

What Kurt heard in The Vaselines was the same thing he heard in Lead Belly and Daniel Johnston: directness. Songs that said the thing they meant without decoration. The Vaselines wrote about ordinary things with a tenderness that Nirvana absorbed and amplified by about a thousand percent.

The Beatles

Kurt loved The Beatles before he loved punk. He said this clearly. Meet the Beatles! was the album he listened to the entire day before writing "About a Girl." He never stopped listening to them. He kept a copy of Revolver in his car.

The Beatles influence in Nirvana is not always obvious because it's filtered through so many other things — hardcore, metal, Sub Pop production. But it is in the melodies. Kurt could write a hook the way very few people can, and the place he learned that was The Beatles.
