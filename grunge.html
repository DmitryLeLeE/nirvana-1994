The Seattle Scene

the world Nirvana came from — rain, flannel, and a label called Sub Pop

The city

Seattle in the late 1980s was far away from everything: rainy, cheap, and full of bands nobody else cared about. That distance was the whole point. You could be loud and weird there because nobody was watching.

The sound they shared — heavy, slow, built on old punk and old metal — got called "grunge." The word was half a joke at first. Then it wasn't.

Sub Pop

The label Sub Pop, founded in 1988 by Bruce Pavitt and Jonathan Poneman, put out the early singles and albums of the whole scene — Nirvana, Soundgarden, Mudhoney, Tad. Their slogan said it plainly: "World Domination."

The look

Flannel shirts from the thrift store, ripped jeans, long hair that nobody styled. It wasn't a fashion — until the magazines made it one. That was the tragedy of it: the scene that hated costumes got turned into a costume.

Sub Pop 200 and C/Z Records

The compilation Sub Pop 200 (1988) was the document of the scene before the scene had a name. Three vinyl records in a gatefold sleeve, with Nirvana, Soundgarden, Mudhoney, Tad, Screaming Trees, and seventeen other bands. The packaging included a photo essay of Seattle. It sold modestly. It was later recognized as the founding document of everything that followed.

C/Z Records, the other Seattle indie, put out the companion piece: Deep Six (1986) — six bands, a double LP, the first recording that caught Soundgarden, Melvins, Malfunkshun, Green River, Skin Yard, and U-Men. Green River became Mudhoney and Pearl Jam. Malfunkshun became Mother Love Bone, which became Temple of the Dog, which became Pearl Jam. It was a small world.

The hardcore roots

The Seattle scene did not come from nowhere. It came from hardcore punk — Black Flag, the Dead Kennedys, the Butthole Surfers — mixed with the heaviness of Black Sabbath and the cheapness of whatever secondhand equipment was available. Kurt grew up on Black Flag's Damaged and the Misfits before he ever picked up a guitar.

The connection to Olympia, Washington mattered too. K Records, Calvin Johnson's label, was putting out low-fi, earnest indie pop from bands like Beat Happening. Kurt had a K Records tattoo on his arm. The idea that music could be made without equipment, money, or permission — that it was about the song, not the studio — came partly from that direction.

The end

After Kurt died in 1994, the center of the scene was gone, and the world moved on within a few years. What's left is the music: a handful of bands from a rainy city who changed how rock sounded, and one of them changed everything.

Layne Staley of Alice in Chains died in 2002. Andrew Wood of Mother Love Bone died in 1990, before the explosion. Shannon Hoon of Blind Melon died in 1995. Scott Weiland of Stone Temple Pilots — not from Seattle, but adopted into the scene by press consensus — died in 2015. The 1990s were expensive.
