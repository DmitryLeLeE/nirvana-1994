The World of 1991–1994

what was happening while Nirvana happened to the world


Seattle & flannel — rain, thrift stores, and 1992

You can't understand Nirvana without understanding what they destroyed.

In 1990, the biggest rock bands in America wore spandex, teased their hair to the ceiling, and sang about girls and cars. Radio programmers ran everything. MTV made them stars. Then a band from Aberdeen, Washington put out a seven-dollar single on a small label in Seattle, and within fourteen months, all of that was gone.

MTV: the machine that made and owned them

In 1991, MTV was the only place that mattered for music. There was no internet. You waited for your video to come on, or you watched 120 Minutes at midnight hoping to catch something strange. When "Smells Like Teen Spirit" hit rotation in September 1991, it went from alternative to everywhere in about three weeks.

MTV's relationship with Nirvana was complicated from the start. At the 1992 Video Music Awards, MTV told the band not to play "Rape Me." Kurt opened the set with its opening chords anyway — held them for a few seconds — then switched to "Lithium." It was the most expensive four seconds in MTV history.

The Unplugged taping on November 18, 1993, was MTV's format — acoustic, stripped back, intimate — but Nirvana made it into something else. The stage was dressed with stargazer lilies and candles. Kurt said he wanted it to look like a funeral. The network executives were uncomfortable. The audience was silent in a way that wasn't awkward. It was one of the greatest live recordings ever made, and it aired on MTV first.

Lollapalooza and the festival circuit

Perry Farrell of Jane's Addiction started Lollapalooza in 1991 as a traveling festival for artists who didn't fit anywhere else — Nine Inch Nails, Butthole Surfers, Ice-T, Siouxsie and the Banshees on the same bill. It was the first festival that felt like it was for people who hated corporate rock.

Nirvana were offered the headlining slot in 1992. Kurt turned it down. He said he didn't want to play that long, in that heat, that many nights in a row. Some say the real reason was that he was already exhausted by the machine that famous had become. Pearl Jam headlined instead.

The festival Nirvana did headline — twice — was Reading in England. 1991 and 1992. The 1992 set is legendary: Kurt arrived in a wheelchair, pretending to be too ill to play, then stood up and tore into "Breed." The crowd went to pieces. It is still discussed as one of the greatest festival sets ever played.

Hair metal: the thing they killed

In 1990, the five biggest rock acts in America were Poison, Warrant, Skid Row, Whitesnake, and Motley Crue. They had combined album sales in the hundreds of millions. Radio stations played them constantly. Rolling Stone put them on covers.

By the end of 1992, most of them had disappeared from radio. Their videos stopped getting played. Record labels stopped signing new bands that sounded like them. Nirvana did not plan this. It happened because "Smells Like Teen Spirit" arrived and everyone who was tired of the other thing finally had something to point at.

Kurt was uncomfortable with this narrative. He said in interviews that he had nothing against those bands personally — he just wrote songs about different things. That may be true. But it doesn't change what happened.

Sub Pop Records: the label that started it

Bruce Pavitt and Jonathan Poneman founded Sub Pop in Seattle in 1988. Their slogan was "World Domination" — partly ironic, partly not. They put out the early Nirvana singles, the first Soundgarden records, Mudhoney, Tad, Screaming Trees.

Their aesthetic was deliberate: grainy photographs, raw production, packaging that looked handmade. The Seattle press was calling it "the Seattle sound" before anyone else used the word grunge. Sub Pop ran a Singles Club — a mail subscription that sent you new 7-inch records every month — which is how "Love Buzz," Nirvana's debut single, reached most of its first listeners.

By 1992, the label was nearly broke. The major labels were poaching everything that mattered. Geffen bought 49% of Sub Pop for $20 million in 1994. The logo — that simple "SUB POP" in block letters — became a fashion item, printed on t-shirts worn by people who had never heard a note of any band on the label.

The flannel question

Flannel shirts were workwear. In Aberdeen, Washington, men wore them to cut timber. In Seattle in the late 1980s, bands wore them because they were cheap, warm, and available at any thrift store for two dollars. It was not a fashion statement.

In October 1992, the New York Times ran a piece about "grunge fashion." A journalist called a Sub Pop employee named Megan Jasper and asked her to provide a glossary of "grunge slang." Jasper made up every single word on the spot. The Times published them as fact. Terms like "cob nobbler" (loser) and "lamestain" (unhip person) appeared in the newspaper of record. Nobody in Seattle used any of them.

Perry Ellis showed "grunge-inspired" flannel on the runway. It cost $600. The original shirt cost $2. This is how most cultural appropriation works, and everyone involved in the scene hated it completely.

Courtney Love & Hole

Hole was not a Nirvana side project. Pretty on the Inside (1991) came out before Nevermind and had its own audience — harsher, more confrontational, Bikini Kill and Babes in Toyland territory. Courtney Love was a musician before she was Kurt Cobain's wife, and the tabloid press spent years trying to erase that fact.

Live Through This was released April 8, 1994 — three days after Kurt died. The timing was horrific and accidental. Critics who were prepared to dismiss it as a vanity project instead had to reckon with the fact that it was one of the best rock albums of the year. It still holds up.

Coffee, rain, and geography

Seattle in 1991 had more coffee shops per capita than any city in America. Starbucks was local — six locations, not yet the empire it became. The rain was not romantic to the people who lived there; it was just the weather. Aberdeen, an hour south, was a timber town with a collapsed economy and not much else. Kurt slept under a bridge there at seventeen. He wrote a song about it.

The geography mattered because it meant the scene developed in isolation. Nobody in Los Angeles or New York was paying attention until it was too late to manage. By the time the major labels arrived, the music was already made.

The Cobain Journals

Kurt kept notebooks his whole life — journals full of song ideas, drawings, rants, shopping lists, fan mail responses he never sent, story fragments, and pages of his own handwriting that look like a mind that never stopped moving. Riverhead Books published a selection of them in 2002, photocopied directly from the originals. Reading them feels like reading over someone's shoulder.

The journals contain some of the most honest writing about the experience of unexpected fame — the hatred of it, the guilt about it, the inability to enjoy it. Kurt was not a simple person who felt simple things. The notebooks make that plain.

"I am not well-read, but when I do read, I read well."
— Kurt Cobain, Journals, 2002

The Internet in 1994: alt.music.nirvana & 14.4k modems

In April 1994, almost nobody had a website. You got online through a 14.4k dial-up modem — the screech of the handshake, twenty minutes to download a single scanned photograph, and a phone line your parents yelled at you to get off.

Where Nirvana fans actually gathered was Usenet: the newsgroup alt.music.nirvana. It was pure plain text. People traded guitar tabs typed in ASCII, argued over bootleg setlists, and posted lyrics from memory. On April 8, 1994, word of Kurt's death spread through alt.music.nirvana faster than most newspapers could print it — kids in college computer labs and bedrooms staring at green-and-black terminals, asking if it was a hoax until the Seattle Times confirmed it.

If you wanted to browse the brand-new World Wide Web, you used NCSA Mosaic or text-only Lynx. Most fan pages lived on university servers (~username/nirvana.html) until places like Geocities came along. Every link you clicked took five seconds to draw a gray background and a 600-pixel banner.

Zines, mixtapes, and hidden tracks

Before blogs, culture traveled on photocopied paper and 90-minute cassettes. Punk and riot grrrl zines — Flipside, Maximumrocknroll, Bikini Kill, and even mainstream-adjacent Sassy — were stapled at Kinko's at midnight and sold for a dollar or two at record stores. That was where you read real interviews, found out which bands were touring, and spotted ads for tape traders.

At home, music lived on TDK SA90 and Maxell XLII cassettes. You dubbed albums off a friend's CD, made mixtapes with handwritten J-cards, and kept a pencil nearby to wind loose tape back into the shell. If a tape got eaten by a car stereo, that was a minor tragedy.

Even CDs had secrets. On early pressings of Nevermind, after "Something in the Way" ended, the disc went silent for ten minutes. If you forgot to hit stop — sitting in your room reading a zine or falling asleep — track 12 suddenly exploded into "Endless, Nameless", six minutes of feedback and screaming that sounded like the studio catching fire.

Magazine covers that defined the era

In 1992, if a band landed on the cover of a magazine, it meant something. Kurt hated the music press, and showed up for the photoshoots anyway — usually to sabotage them.
