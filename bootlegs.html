Bootlegs & Rarities

recordings that exist outside the official catalog — the ones that traveled by cassette

Before the internet, bootlegs moved through tape trading networks. You'd find someone in a zine ad, send them a blank TDK and a stamped return envelope, and six weeks later a cassette would appear in your mailbox with handwritten track lists. The recordings were often fifth-generation copies. They sounded like the past heard through a wall. That was fine. That was the point.

The Peel Sessions

John Peel's late-night BBC Radio 1 show was where bands from the American underground came when they toured England. Nirvana recorded two sessions for Peel:

SessionRecordedTracks
Peel Session #1 October 26, 1989 "Love Buzz," "About a Girl," "Spank Thru," "Polly"
Peel Session #2 November 3, 1991 "Drain You," "Aneurysm," "Even in His Youth," "Turnaround"

The 1989 session was recorded while they were touring on a budget of almost nothing, staying in strangers' houses and eating gas station food. The performances are raw and direct in a way the studio albums never quite captured. "Polly" here is especially striking — just Kurt and an acoustic guitar, no production at all.

Smart Studios demos (1990)

In April 1990, before Dave Grohl joined, the band recorded demos at Smart Studios in Madison, Wisconsin with producer Butch Vig. These sessions became the foundation of Nevermind. The demos include early versions of "In Bloom," "Lithium," "Breed" (then called "Imodium"), and "Polly."

The difference between the demos and the final album is dramatic. The demos are louder and messier, with Chad Channing on drums. Butch Vig later said he knew immediately that he was hearing something exceptional, and that he thought the band needed a different drummer.

These recordings circulated widely among collectors and were eventually included in the 2004 box set With the Lights Out.

The Boombox Rehearsals (1988)

The earliest Nirvana recordings that exist were made on a consumer boombox at practice sessions in 1988. The tape quality is exactly what you would expect: overdriven, distant, everything bleeding together. Kurt was 21. The songs are rough drafts of things that would appear later in polished form, and several that never appeared anywhere else.

What is remarkable is how present Kurt's voice already is. It has the same quality it has on In Utero — that specific combination of beauty and damage. The recordings were released officially in With the Lights Out (2004).

MTV Unplugged: what wasn't on the album

The official Unplugged in New York album (released November 1, 1994) contains fourteen tracks. The full taping included additional material that didn't make the cut. Two different sets were taped before the main show — a sound check and a full rehearsal — and recordings from these have circulated in collector communities.

The official release omits "Dumb," which was performed during the main set, and cuts the in-between stage banter and tuning sections. The bootleg versions that circulate include these elements and give a fuller picture of how the evening felt in the room.

What the official release captures exactly right is the setlist choice. Kurt insisted on playing six covers. The band opened with "About a Girl" and closed with Lead Belly's "In the Pines" / "Where Did You Sleep Last Night." It was a very deliberate statement about where the music came from.

Reading Festival 1992

August 30, 1992. Kurt arrived at the Reading stage in a wheelchair pushed by Krist, wearing a hospital gown and a blond wig. This was a response to press reports that he was too ill to perform. He stood up, removed the wig, and launched into "Breed."

The set that followed is considered one of the finest live performances in rock history. The band played 24 songs in front of 70,000 people. Kurt's voice was at its most powerful. The bootleg recording that circulated on cassette for years captured it all. A professional broadcast version was eventually released officially in 2009.

What the bootleg version has that the official release doesn't: the crowd noise between songs, the moments of tuning, the feedback, the sense of a band playing as if something depended on it.

"You Know You're Right" (1994)

The last song Nirvana ever recorded was taped at Robert Lang Studios in Seattle on January 30, 1994. It stayed unreleased for eight years.

The official release came in 2002 as part of the Nirvana best-of compilation. A bootleg version — slightly different mix, different mastering — had circulated in collector communities since 1994 under the title "Oh Me Oh My" and other names. It is one of the most hunted recordings in rock bootleg history.

The song itself is about pain. The vocal is some of the most extreme Kurt ever put on tape. The last line is "pain." He sings it until it isn't a word anymore.

The In Utero alternate mix

Steve Albini produced In Utero in two weeks at Pachyderm Studio in Minnesota in February 1993. DGC Records was unhappy with the result — too raw, too abrasive, unplayable on mainstream radio. Scott Litt was brought in to remix two tracks: "Heart-Shaped Box" and "All Apologies."

The Albini mixes circulate in collector communities and are significantly different from the album. The bass is heavier, the vocals are more exposed, and the whole thing sounds like it was recorded in one room with people who had something to say and no patience for anyone who wanted them to say it differently.

Albini has said in interviews that he doesn't particularly care which version people prefer. He made what the band asked him to make.

The tape trader's ethics

In 1992, the rules of tape trading were clear: you traded tape for tape, never charged money, credited your sources, and noted the generation of the copy. A "first-generation" tape was made directly from the original recording. A "third-generation" tape was a copy of a copy of a copy, and you could hear it.

People who sold bootlegs for profit were called "boots sellers" and were regarded with contempt by most serious collectors.

The ethics held because everyone was doing it for the music.
